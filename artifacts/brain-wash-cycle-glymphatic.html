Your brain has no lymphatic vessels. So where does the waste go?

Every other organ in your body uses lymphatic vessels — a network of drain pipes — to flush out cellular waste. The brain is sealed off behind the blood-brain barrier, locked in a rigid skull with no room for plumbing. Yet somehow, it stays clean.

The glymphatic system — named for the glial cells that power it — was discovered in 2012 by Maiken Nedergaard's lab at the University of Rochester. Cerebrospinal fluid rushes along the outside of arteries, then forces itself through brain tissue, picking up metabolic waste before draining out along veins. The brain invented its own washing machine.

At peak slow-wave sleep, glial cells (especially astrocytes) contract, expanding the interstitial space by up to 60%. The wider channel lets cerebrospinal fluid flood through, sweeping amyloid-β, tau, and α-synuclein out toward the meningeal lymphatics at the skull boundary. Discovered by Xie et al., Science 2013.

What if Alzheimer's isn't just a disease — but a slow drainage failure?

Amyloid-β is cleared primarily through the glymphatic system. One night of sleep deprivation measurably raises amyloid-β in the human brain (reported by Shokri-Kojori et al., PNAS 2018). Pull the sliders and watch what chronic sleep loss does to the same neurons over time.
